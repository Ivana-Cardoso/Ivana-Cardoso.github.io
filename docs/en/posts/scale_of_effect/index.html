What is the scale of effect and how do we identify it?

R
landscape ecology
Author

Ivana Cardoso

Published

February 16, 2025

What is scale?

Scale is a central concept in research planning and in the interpretation of ecological patterns. It defines the lens through which we observe natural phenomena and is made up of two key elements:

  • Grain: the smallest unit measured, or the size of the sampling units;

  • Extent: the total area covered by the study, or the duration of a time series.

The spatial scale at which landscape characteristics are measured can affect the inferred relationships (Wiens, 1989). For example, in a study of 12 beetle species, the correlation between forest cover and abundance ranged from positive to nonexistent, depending on the scale at which forest cover was measured (Holland et al., 2004). In addition, each species interacts with the environment in a different way. For example, an analysis of forest cover in landscapes with a 2 km radius revealed opposite effects on different groups of birds: forest specialist, frugivorous, and insectivorous birds had their diversity reduced in areas with lower forest cover. In contrast, generalist birds showed greater diversity in those same areas (Morante-Filho et al., 2015). Thus, what may have little effect on some species at a given scale can be catastrophic for others. This requires adjustments in the sampling design to avoid mistaken conclusions, since inadequate scales may fail to reveal ecological relationships or create false patterns. For this reason, it is often necessary to test different scales to find the scale of effect.

What is the scale of effect?

The scale of effect is the spatial scale at which a given landscape characteristic best explains the response variable (Jackson and Fahrig, 2015). It can be determined by following the steps below:

  • In data collection: measuring the response variable in several sampling units across a region (Fig. 1a);

  • In the multi-scale analysis: measuring the landscape characteristic of interest at multiple spatial extents around each sampling unit (Fig. 1b);

  • In the statistical analysis: identifying the scale with the highest correlation coefficient, regression slope, or model information value between the predictor variable of interest, related to landscape structure, and the response variable of interest (Fig. 1c).

The selection of spatial scales should take into account the biology of the studied species. For example, in a study of Amazonian understory birds that tested 40 intervals, the smallest scale (50 m) was defined as intermediate between these birds’ reluctance to cross 30 m gaps and their ability to cross gaps of up to 70 m. The largest scale (2,000 m) corresponded to the value frequently used in habitat fragmentation studies (Bueno and Peres, 2019). Finally, besides reflecting the biology of the species, it is essential to test different intervals and to consider that the scale of effect identified in multi-scale analyses may be imprecise if the scales are:

  • Too small or too large;

  • Limited in range (i.e., only intermediate sizes);

  • Insufficient in number (i.e., too few scales tested).

How do we calculate the scale of effect?

For this exercise, we will use the data from Bueno and Peres (2019). I chose this study because the authors calculated forest cover at different spatial scales and identified the scale of effect. This allows us to compare our approach with that of the study, ensuring that we are calculating correctly.

In the supplementary material of the paper, the authors provide the code used in the analyses and in the generation of the study’s graphs. This code begins with data processing and results in the table below (birds). If you prefer, you can access the supplementary material, download the original tables, and reprocess the data manually. For this exercise, we are interested in finding out which scale of effect best explains the relationship between insectivorous bird species richness and landscape forest cover. Therefore, the first step is to transform the abundance data into richness data:

# Calculates insectivorous bird species richness in each sampling unit
islands$richness = rowSums(ifelse(birds > 0, 1, 0))

Determining the scale of effect using correlation

In the study by Bueno and Peres (2019), the predictor (forest cover) and response (number of species) variables are transformed by log10(x + 1) for all analyses. Therefore, we will also use the transformed variables to calculate the scale of effect. Remember to pay attention to how you are using your variables in the analyses and make sure to include them correctly when calculating the scale of effect. To calculate the scale of effect using correlation, we will use the following command format:

object name <- cor.test(predictor variable of interest, response variable of interest)

For example, to calculate the correlation between landscape forest cover at a scale of 50 meters and species richness we will use:

cor50 <- cor.test(log10(islands$forest.cover.50 + 1), log10(islands$richness + 1))
cor50$estimate
      cor 
0.7072203

Since we need to do this for 40 scales, from 50 m to 2000 m at 50 m intervals, we will create a for loop and store the results in a table.

# Creating a vector with the desired scales
escalas <- seq(50, 2000, by = 50)

# Creating a data frame to store the results
resultados <- data.frame(Escala = numeric(), Correlacao = numeric(), Pvalor = numeric())

# Loop to calculate the correlation at each scale
for (escala in escalas) {
  # Creating the column name dynamically
  nome_coluna <- paste0("forest.cover.", escala)
  
  # Checking whether the column exists in the data frame
  if (nome_coluna %in% names(islands)) {
    # Calculating the Pearson correlation
    cor_test <- cor.test(log10(islands[[nome_coluna]]+1), log10(islands$richness+1))
    
    # Saving the results in the data frame
    resultados <- rbind(resultados, data.frame(
      Escala = escala,
      Correlacao = cor_test$estimate,
      Pvalor = cor_test$p.value
    ))
  }
}

Conclusion

As I mentioned earlier, there are different ways to determine the scale of effect, depending on the research goal and the analysis used. Therefore, the first step is to choose the most appropriate analysis, considering the question, the data collected, and the study design. Then, we apply this analysis at different scales and identify the one at which the relationship between the response and predictor variables is strongest, that is, the scale of effect.
