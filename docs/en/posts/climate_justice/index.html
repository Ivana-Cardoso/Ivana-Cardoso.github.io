Is there hope for a climate-just world?

mudanças climáticas
política
Author

Ivana Cardoso

Published

November 15, 2026

When we talk about justice, we assume that an injustice exists. In today’s post, I’ll be discussing climate injustice, how it happens, and who it affects. I chose this topic because of the 31st Conference of the Parties (COP31), taking place in Antalya, Turkey, until November 20, at a time when global warming is intensifying and extreme weather events are becoming increasingly frequent. Before answering the question in the title, there’s another question that guides this post, and one I’d like you to think about as well: Who will pay the price for extreme weather?

Perhaps you, reader, still remember the floods that hit Rio Grande do Sul in 2023 and, even more severely, in 2024, when entire cities were submerged, thousands of people were displaced, and hundreds of lives were lost. During those same years, I was in Manaus, more than 3,000 kilometers away, witnessing the exact opposite. The drought in northern Brazil was worsening, and river levels dropped week after week. With too little water to navigate, many riverside communities became isolated, with their access to drinking water and food security compromised. On top of that came heat waves and smoke from wildfires, which blanketed the city for weeks. In the same country, in the very same year, regions at opposite ends faced extreme weather events: too much water in one place and too little in another.

Events like these have become more frequent and intense due to human activities. The burning of fossil fuels, deforestation, and agriculture are major sources of greenhouse gas emissions, contributing to global warming, which in turn intensifies extreme weather events such as droughts, floods, and heat waves. What these events have in common is that they hit hardest those who have contributed the least to causing them. This is called climate injustice. Closely linked to climate injustice is environmental racism, which occurs when the risks and impacts of these climate events disproportionately affect the most vulnerable segments of the population. In other words, those who pay the price for extreme weather have a race and a ZIP code. As Marina Silva, then Brazil’s Minister of the Environment and Climate Change, put it:

Environmental racism is the heaviest price that vulnerable communities pay when extreme weather events occur or when environmental conditions become precarious. This is because the poorest people, women, Black people, Indigenous peoples, […] and quilombola communities are precisely those who receive the least support from the government to protect their health, access credit, receive technical assistance, and have access to adequate infrastructure and housing in safe locations. Instead, they are pushed onto hillsides, along riverbanks and streams, and into areas that are simply not suitable for living […]. When flash floods occur, these are the people who pay the highest price, sometimes even with their lives.

This social inequality, and the environmental racism associated with it, are no coincidence. They are rooted in the economic system we live under, capitalism, and its connection to our colonial history. The colonial system divided people along racial lines and determined where each group could live, based on their perceived usefulness for the extraction of wealth. The legacy of this system persists to this day in the unequal distribution of environmental risks, which follows the same lines as income and race. Forced into unsuitable and high-risk areas, Black and low-income communities also face a lack of access to essential public services.

The same logic applies between countries. Capitalism and colonialism are deeply interconnected, and wealthy countries built much of their populations’ prosperity through the extraction of wealth during the colonial era. This logic of capitalist extraction has affected not only territories and peoples, but also nature itself, which, within the capitalist worldview, is treated as something separate from humanity and as a resource to be exploited to sustain production and generate profit. As a result, wealthy countries, which have historically been the largest emitters of greenhouse gases, are also the most responsible for climate change. Yet, they are also the countries best equipped to protect their populations from its impacts

NoteA brief history of the COPs

The first United Nations Conference on the Human Environment was held in June 1972 in Stockholm, Sweden. It encouraged countries to monitor environmental conditions and establish environmental ministries and agencies. Twenty years later, the Rio-92 (also known as the Eco-92), held on Rio de Janeiro, brought together representatives from 178 countries, including 108 heads of state and government.

At the Rio-92, countries adopted the Declaração do Rio, which established the principle of common but differentiated responsibilities. Under this principle, wealthier countries, which have contributed the most to the problem, are expected to take the lead in addressing it and support developing nations. The summit also opened two major international agreements for signature: the United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change (UNFCCC), aimed at addressing climate change, and the Convention on Biological Diversity (CDB), focused on biodiversity conservation and the sustainable use of its resources. Both conventions established international commitments and provided for the creation of the Conference of the Parties (COP), where countries meet periodically to assess progress, negotiate new commitments, and discuss how to implement the agreements. And that’s how the COPs came to be. The first COP was held in 1995.

After more than three decades, the COPs have led to important agreements and institutional progress, including the Kyoto Protocol (1997) and the Paris Agreement (2015). However, there is still a significant gap between the commitments countries make and their actual implementation. Climate finance is one example. The goal of providing US$ 100 billion per year, pledged by wealthy countries in 2009, was only reached in 2022, two years behind schedule. Even then, this amount represents only a fraction of what is actually needed and much of the funding comes in the form of loans, rather than grants.

Today, one of the key issues under discussion is the phase-out of fossil fuels. Brazil, which hosted COP30 and presented roadmaps for moving away from fossil fuels and ending deforestation, also authorized Petrobras to drill exploratory wells at the mouth of the Amazon River, off the coast of Amapá.

Is there hope?

Before answering, I should clarify that my perspective depends on the lens through which I see the world. You, reader, may see things differently, and I’d be happy to hear your thoughts in the comments.

In my view, there is little hope, and it comes with conditions. Tackling climate injustice and environmental racism requires more than individual actions. We need concrete public policies developed with the participation of Black, low-income, Indigenous, and marginalized communities, the very people most affected, and implemented where they are needed most. We need to adapt our cities, making them greener and more resilient to heat, while also creating sponge cities, designed to absorb and drain rainwater. We also need strong public pressure around environmental issues, both at home and at international events such as COP31. Global political leaders must be held accountable and, above all, take steps to gradually reduce fossil fuel use, strengthen environmental laws, and strictly punish illegal deforestation, among other measures. In the words of Swedish climate activist Greta Thunberg:

Of course, it is absolutely essential that we keep doing everything within our power, because this is an opportunity to mobilize people […]. We are here, as activists, to put pressure on those in power and push them to finally put into practice what they have been saying for decades and decades.

I believe that, if the conditions I’ve mentioned above are met, we can reduce the impacts of climate change. However, a climate-just world may remain out of reach as long as our economic system continues to prioritize unchecked profit. That’s why I believe the path toward a climate-just world begins with recognizing capitalism as the root cause of the current climate crisis and moving toward an ecosocialist transition. Ecosocialism advocates for a transformation of society that reshapes how we produce goods and generate energy, embraces international solidarity, and puts an end to the exploitation of human beings by capital and of nature by the pursuit of profit.

Returning to where this post began, I talked about a country that, in the same year, faced extreme weather events in opposite regions, and I asked who would pay the price. Today, the answer remains the same: those who pay the highest price are still those who contributed the least to the problem. Changing this reality depends on public policies, international agreements, and constant pressure from society. Hope may be scarce, but it is not gone. It grows with every person who refuses to accept the climate crisis and the injustice it brings as normal.

And you, reader, what do you think needs to be done to build a climate-just world?
